# Lesson 1: The Question Behind the Question

**Bottom line.** A lawyer does not answer the question you asked. A lawyer finds the real issue under it, names the rule that decides it, applies the facts, and only then gives a conclusion. Learn to do that first step yourself, and every meeting with a professional gets shorter and cheaper.

## In plain English
When most people ask a legal question, they ask it as a yes-or-no. A lawyer hears a bundle of smaller questions hiding inside the big one. The habit has four parts: find the issue, find the rule, apply the facts, conclude. Lawyers call this IRAC.

## The framework
1. Issue: rewrite the question so it names what is actually at stake.
2. Rule: name the source that decides each piece. Unknown source = first task, not a reason to guess.
3. Application: put your real facts against the rule.
4. Conclusion: "yes, if…" with every condition listed.
Plus the reflex question: who has the power to decide this?

## A worked example
Hypothetical: a developer owns a lot personally and asks "can I just put the land into my new company?" Four issues (existence, cost, consent, regulator's view of ownership), four rulebooks, facts decide, conclusion is a sequence of steps.
Professional Sign-Off Required: real estate counsel in the land's jurisdiction; tax advisor in each country.

## The beginner mistake
Accepting the question as asked, then acting on a "yes" that was missing three "ifs".

## Checklist
Rewrite the question · list every rulebook · mark facts known/assumed/unverified · ask who decides · conclude "yes, if…" · name the sign-off.

## Self-test
"Can my company lend me money to buy a house?" Write the four IRAC lines. Model answer on the page.

## Apply it today
Take one live question, write it as asked, then write the IRAC version, bring it to the next professional meeting.

## Sources and sign-offs

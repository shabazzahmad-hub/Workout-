# Lesson 2: Facts First: Fact, Assumption, Inference, Conclusion

**Bottom line.** Most legal and business mistakes do not come from a wrong rule. They come from a "fact" that was never a fact. Sort every statement into four boxes before you rely on it: fact, assumption, inference, conclusion. Then act only on what the boxes can carry.

## In plain English

Lawyers are trained to ask one question of every sentence: how do you know? "The bank approved it" might mean the bank signed a letter, or someone said so on a call, or you hope so. Those are three different things with three different weights.

## The framework

- **Fact.** Something you can prove with a document, a record or a witness. "The lease is dated 1 March," with the lease in hand.
- **Assumption.** Something you believe but have not checked. "The permit covers the whole lot."
- **Inference.** A step of reasoning from facts. "The deed names one owner, so one signature is probably enough." It is only as strong as the facts and the logic under it.
- **Conclusion.** The decision you reach. It inherits the weakest box beneath it.

Rule of thumb: every fact carries a source and a date. Every assumption carries an owner and a check. A conclusion built on an unchecked assumption must say so.

## A worked example

Hypothetical numbers. A developer plans to buy a lot for 400,000 and says: "The seller owns it outright, the zoning allows four units, and the sale closes in 30 days."

- "Owns it outright" is an assumption. It becomes a fact only after an official title search shows no mortgage or claim.
- "Zoning allows four units" is an assumption. It becomes a fact only after the planning authority confirms in writing.
- "Closes in 30 days" is an inference from the seller's wish, not a fact.

The conclusion as first stated: "buy now." The conclusion after sorting: "Buy, if the title search is clean, if zoning is confirmed in writing, and if the closing date is set in the agreement."

Suppose the zoning is wrong and allows only two units. At an assumed value of 150,000 per unit (hypothetical), four units hold 600,000 of value and two units hold 300,000. The unchecked assumption was worth 300,000 and cost a one-page letter to test.

Professional Sign-Off Required: real estate counsel for title; a planning or land-use professional for zoning.

## The beginner mistake

Treating a confident tone as proof. A fact repeated by three people may still trace to one unchecked source. Another common slip: writing assumptions in the same sentence style as facts, so that a week later nobody can tell them apart.

## Checklist

- List every statement your decision depends on.
- Label each: fact, assumption, or inference.
- Attach a source and a date to each fact.
- Attach an owner and a deadline to each assumption.
- Check the one assumption that moves the most money first.
- Write the conclusion as "yes, if…" using the unchecked items.

## Self-test

A friend says: "My company can lend me 50,000. My accountant said it was fine last year, and the shareholders will not mind." Sort the three statements.

**Show a model answer**

"Accountant said it was fine last year" is a remembered statement, not a document, and it concerned last year. It is an assumption until you hold it in writing for this loan. "Shareholders will not mind" is a prediction: an assumption, with a check, a signed resolution. "The company can lend 50,000" is a conclusion resting on both. Professional Sign-Off Required: accountant or tax advisor, in writing, dated.

## Apply it today

Take one decision pending this week. Write a two-column page. On the left, what you know, with source and date. On the right, what you are assuming, with a name and a check. Do not sign anything until the right column has an owner on every line.

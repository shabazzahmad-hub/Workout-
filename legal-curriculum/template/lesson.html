Lesson 1: The Question Behind the Question

Lesson 1 of 56About 10 minutesTuesday, October 27, 2026
Tap Listen to hear the lesson read aloud.

Bottom line. A lawyer does not answer the question you asked. A lawyer finds the real issue under it, names the rule that decides it, applies the facts, and only then gives a conclusion. Learn to do that first step yourself, and every meeting with a professional gets shorter and cheaper.

In plain English

When most people ask a legal question, they ask it as a yes-or-no. "Can I move my land into my company?" "Can I pay my brother from the Holdco?" A lawyer hears something different. A lawyer hears a bundle of smaller questions hiding inside the big one.

Here is a comparison. A doctor does not answer "can I run a marathon?" with yes or no. The doctor asks about your heart, your knees, and your training. Then the doctor answers a better question: "what would have to be true for a marathon to be safe for you?" Thinking like a lawyer is the same move.

The habit has four parts. Find the issue, the real thing in dispute or at risk. Find the rule, the law or contract term that decides it. Apply the rule to your exact facts. Then conclude. Lawyers call this IRAC. It is the oldest tool in legal training, and it works for business decisions too.

The framework

IRAC stands for Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion. Run it in that order, every time.

  1. Issue. Rewrite the question so it names what is actually at stake. "Can I transfer the land?" becomes "What does a transfer trigger: tax, fees, consents, and who must agree?"
  2. Rule. Name the source that decides each piece. A statute, a regulation, a contract clause, a bank covenant, a trust deed. If you do not know the source, that is your first task, not a reason to guess.
  3. Application. Put your real facts against the rule. Not the facts you wish you had. The land is in one name, the company may not yet exist, the bank may hold a charge. Each fact changes the answer.
  4. Conclusion. Now give the answer, with its conditions attached. "Yes, if the company exists, if the lender consents, and if the tax cost is acceptable." A conclusion without conditions is a guess.

Add one more question that lawyers ask by reflex: who has the power to decide this? A regulator, a lender, a co-owner, a trustee, a court. If the answer is "someone other than me", your real task is to satisfy that person, not to convince yourself.

A worked example

Hypothetical. A developer owns a building lot in her own name. She asks: "Can I just put the land into my new company?"

Issue. The real issues are: does the company exist yet; what does the transfer cost in tax and government fees; does any lender or co-owner have to agree; and does moving the land change who the regulator thinks is the owner.

Rule. Four different sources decide those four issues: company law for existence, tax law for the cost, the mortgage document for consent, and the planning authority's own rules for ownership. One question, four rulebooks.

Application. Suppose the company is not yet registered. Then the answer to the whole question is "not yet", whatever the tax rules say. Suppose it is registered, but a bank holds a mortgage that says "no transfer without our written consent". Then the next step is a letter to the bank, not a deed.

Conclusion. "Yes, once the company exists, with the bank's written consent, after a tax professional has priced the transfer in both countries." Notice that the conclusion is a sequence of steps, not a word.

Professional Sign-Off Required: real estate counsel in the land's jurisdiction, and a tax advisor in each country involved, before any transfer document is signed.

The beginner mistake

Accepting the question as it was asked. A yes-or-no question invites a yes-or-no answer, and a yes-or-no answer hides every condition that matters. The second mistake follows from the first: acting on the "yes" before anyone checked the conditions. Most expensive legal problems start with a true "yes" that was missing three "ifs".

Checklist

  • Rewrite the question so it names what is at risk.
  • List every rulebook that touches it. Expect more than one.
  • Write your real facts down. Mark each one: known, assumed, or unverified.
  • Ask who has the power to decide. Plan to satisfy that person.
  • State the conclusion as "yes, if…" with every condition listed.
  • Name which licensed professional must sign off before you act.

Self-test

A friend asks you: "Can my company lend me money to buy a house?" Before you answer, write the four IRAC lines.

Show a model answer

Issue: Not "can it lend" but "what does a loan from a company to its owner trigger: tax on the owner, rules on repayment timing, and what the company's own documents allow."

Rule: Tax law on shareholder loans, the company's articles and any shareholder agreement, and any bank covenant that limits related-party loans. [Specific rules unverified — confirm independently.]

Application: Depends on the repayment period, whether interest is charged, and whether the company's lender must consent.

Conclusion: "Possibly, if it is repaid within the period the tax rules allow, documented as a real loan, and not blocked by the bank." Professional Sign-Off Required: tax advisor.

Apply it today

Take one live question on your desk this week. Write it the way you first thought it. Under it, write the IRAC version: the issue, the rulebooks, your facts marked known or unverified, and a "yes, if…" conclusion. Bring that page to your next professional meeting. You will spend the meeting on the conditions, not on the question.
